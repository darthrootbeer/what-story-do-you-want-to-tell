A Question of Proportion

The beanstalk was not metaphorical.

Jack was five feet tall and weighed perhaps nine stone, which is to say he was approximately the size of the Giant's thumb. He had not thought about this going in. He was thinking about the harp, which was gold, and the hen, which laid golden eggs, and the bag of gold coins, which was itself approximately the size of Jack. He was not a complicated child. Complicated children do not climb beanstalks.

The Giant's kitchen table was the size of a field. The chair legs were the size of oak trees. The Giant's left boot, abandoned by the door, contained enough cubic footage to house a family of reasonable size — two bedrooms, modest but comfortable, with a fine view of the laces. Jack noted this and filed it away. Jack was always filing things away. This was the only quality that saved him.

"The Giant's footstep shook the valley."

— Jack 5 feet

Above the Clouds

Everything up here is gold.

The castle above the clouds was built for a being whose proportions made the word architecture feel polite. The corridors were designed for something forty feet tall. The fireplaces were the size of houses. The windows — if you could reach them, which Jack could not — looked down on the world through cloud cover, the villages below the size of thumbprints, the rivers thin as thread.

The golden harp stood in the great hall. It was made of gold — real gold, a harp-shaped mass of it — and it played itself. Played the Giant to sleep. Played the clouds into formations. The harp had opinions about what it played and when. Jack understood this immediately. Some objects acquire opinions over centuries. You do not argue with them. You simply listen, and then, if the moment is right, you run.

"Giants is not as bad as human beans think. Giants is not killing each other so very much. Mostly giants is just enormous."

— after Roald Dahl, The BFG

Design Rationale

Typography can be a physical experience.

Ultra is the heaviest weight available from Google Fonts. It fills space the way a Giant fills a room — unavoidably, without asking permission. Placed next to Nunito's airy rounds, it creates a physical sensation: you feel the weight difference before you consciously register it. This is not a typographic trick. It is how scale works. The ratio creates the experience.

Weight is Meaning

Typographic weight is not decoration — it's argument. Ultra-heavy type physically occupies more space, slows the eye, demands acknowledgment. The Giant's words should feel heavy.

Scale Contrast

The power of this aesthetic is the ratio. Tiny human text next to massive giant text creates an experiential gap the reader must cross. That crossing is the story.

Simple Forms

Jack and the Beanstalk survives because it is elemental: big vs. small, earth vs. sky, greed vs. need. Storyteller works with the same raw materials.
