Design System Foyer
You are standing in a design system. The room smells faintly of phosphor and possibility. A large monitor dominates the north wall, its screen glowing green with the cool certainty of a system that knows what it is. Graph paper dungeon maps are pinned to a corkboard nearby. Someone has written "EVERY STORY TALKS BACK" in block capitals along the baseboard.

There is a floppy disk drive here, its amber light blinking steadily.
Obvious exits: NORTH (Typography), SOUTH (Animation), EAST (The Demo), WEST (Components), DOWN (The Parser).

This style concept draws from the canonical aesthetic of Infocom’s golden age — roughly 1980–1989. Green phosphor on black. Monospace everything. The parser prompt as interface philosophy. It is a style that says: the story is the machine, and the machine is friendly if you learn its language.

For Storyteller, this is more than nostalgia. Infocom invented the idea of a story that listens. That responds. That remembers. Storyteller is the heir to that tradition — the design system should make that lineage legible. The glow is not decoration; it is a founding document.

[002] Demo Content
> EXAMINE OCEAN
The Open Sea, 1866
The year is 1866. The sea is restless. Something enormous is out there — something that has terrified sailors from the American coast to the shores of Japan. The newspapers call it a sea monster. The navy calls it a threat to navigation. Professor Aronnax calls it a mystery worth solving.

You are aboard the Abraham Lincoln, a frigate of the United States Navy. The smell of coal smoke and brine is everywhere. The crew moves with purpose. The horizon is empty, and then, suddenly, it is not.
Items visible: Captain's Log, Harpoon Gun, Nautical Charts.
> EXAMINE CREATURE
A phosphorescent mass, enormous and electric, moves beneath the surface. It is longer than any whale recorded in the natural history of the world. It does not breathe. It does not breach. It simply is — a luminous shape in black water, a fact the old sciences have no name for yet.

Ned Land, the Canadian harpooner, is staring at it. He has never missed a target in twenty years. His hand is trembling.
> TAKE HARPOON
You take the harpoon. It is heavier than it looks. Ned Land looks at you with an expression that is equal parts professional pride and mortal unease.
> THROW HARPOON
The harpoon strikes. There is a sound like iron hitting iron — not flesh, not bone, but metal. The creature does not flee. It turns.
> GO NORTH
The deck tilts. The sea takes you. The last thing you see from the surface is the frigate shrinking against the night sky, its lights growing small and then smaller. Then you are underwater, and there is a hatch, and the hatch is open, and inside is light.
Interior — Nautilus, Upper Salon
You are inside the machine that the world mistook for a monster. The salon is paneled in rare wood; the cases hold specimens no museum has catalogued. A pipe organ stands against one wall. Captain Nemo is not here, but his presence is in every object — in the pearl-handled instruments, in the books arranged in seven languages, in the particular green cast of the porthole light as the submarine descends.

The ocean is on the other side of the glass. It is magnificent.
Obvious exits: AFT (Engine Room), FORWARD (Bridge). Obvious objects: Library, Organ, Porthole.
> EXAMINE PORTHOLE
Through the thick glass you can see the deep ocean floor. Strange luminescent creatures drift past — things with no names in any language yet, though Captain Nemo has catalogued them all in his private taxonomy. The light here is blue-green and ancient. You understand, looking through this glass, that the surface world is the small one.

Design Rationale

This design system is built for Storyteller because Storyteller is the heir to Infocom. The parser that talked back. The story that responded. The world that remembered what you did. The green glow is not nostalgia; it is a founding document. When a player sees this aesthetic, they understand immediately: this system was built by people who know where stories come from.

Infocom made something the entertainment industry had never made before: a story that waits for you. Not a film, not a book, not a game — something new that borrowed from all three and owed itself to none. The parser was the grammar of an entire medium. GO NORTH. TAKE LAMP. EXAMINE GRUE. The verbs were the interface, and the interface was invisible when it was working.

Storyteller inherits this. The Narrator is the parser. The Guide is the dungeon master. Storytime is the session. The visual language of this style concept declares that inheritance openly — it is legible to anyone who has ever sat in front of a green screen at 2am, wondering what was north of the trapdoor.

[004] Inventory / Components
> INVENTORY
You are carrying:
  • The Narrator — AI system that runs the world. Knows everything. Says only what serves the story.
  • The Guide — Human interface for corrections. Never breaks Storytime to act.
  • The Play Engine — Resolves actions, tracks state, enforces the rules of the world.
  • The Chronicle — Persistent memory. The story of what has already happened.
  • A TOME Package — Portable story world. Self-contained. Runs anywhere.
  • The Pathfinder — Onboarding system. Learns the player before the first session.

// Narrator response pipeline
function generateResponse(playerAction) {
  const context = chronicle.getRecentMemory();
  const world = playEngine.getCurrentState();
  return narrator.respond({
    action: playerAction,
    context,
    world,
    constraint: "never break storytime"
  });
}
