Style Overview

Before the page,
there was the voice.

Every story was once a performance. Someone stood in firelight, or on a stage, or beneath a lantern, and gave themselves over to the telling. Their voice was the medium. Their body was the interface. Their memory held everything.

This design system is built in honor of that — the griot, the bard, the skald, the storyteller-for-hire who crossed continents with only their voice as a credential. The West African griot who memorized generations of history. Homer reciting the Iliad to a crowd. Shadow puppet wayang performances in Java. The Celtic bard. The Aboriginal songline. Every culture on earth had someone whose job was to hold the stories and give them back.

It is a design language of breath and rhythm, pattern and pause. It says: the story is alive, and so are you.

"The story is not in the book. The story is in the space between the teller and the heard."
— On the Oral Tradition

And so it was that each night, Shahrázád would begin a story, and let it run through the hours of darkness until the first light of dawn appeared on the horizon. At that moment she would cease, the story unfinished, the King's curiosity alive. Style Rationale

Why This Works
for Storyteller

  • Voice is the interface. Storyteller is voice-native. This aesthetic makes that central — the sound wave isn't decoration, it's a literal representation of how the product works. The medium is the message.
  • Universal, not Western. The global oral tradition patterns — Celtic knotwork, kente-inspired stripes, Islamic geometric tiles — signal that stories belong to all of humanity, not just one lineage of storytelling. Storyteller is for everyone.
  • Breath mirrors spoken pacing. The animations move like breath: slow, expansive, never snapping. This mirrors the pacing of spoken narrative — the pause before the next chapter, the inhale before the reveal.
  • Warmth and ritual, not generic. Saffron and crimson evoke firelight, ceremony, the lantern above the stage. They're warm without being sweet. Ritual without being religious. They say: this moment matters.

Demo Content — Story Beats

The Road, the Road,
the Everlasting Road

Kim by Rudyard Kipling, 1901 — Public Domain. Arranged as story beats.

Beat I — The Boy

He sat, in defiance of municipal orders, astride the gun Zam-Zammah on her brick platform opposite the old Ajaib-Gher — the Wonder House, as the natives call the Lahore Museum. Who hold Zam-Zammah, that "fire-breathing dragon," hold the Punjab, for the great green-bronze piece is always first of the conqueror's loot.

Beat II — The Road Calls

Kim was English — though he did not know it. His mother had died of cholera in Ferozepore, his father, a colour-sergeant of an Irish regiment, had died of drink and opium in Lahore. The woman who had looked after him said he was white, though she was half caste. She belonged to the city as Kim belonged to the whole great world.

′

Beat III — The Lama

Then came, from nowhere in particular, an old man, yellow-robed, his head shaved — a Tibetan lama from beyond the Himalayas. He walked slowly as one in a dream, for he was not of this world. He sought the River of the Arrow, a holy river that would wash away all sin. And he met Kim on the steps of the Wonder House. That was the beginning of everything.

Beat IV — The Great Game

"I go to look for the River," said the lama, "and I need a chela — a disciple — to carry my begging bowl and walk with me on the road." And Kim, who had never stayed anywhere longer than a week, who knew every backstreet and alley of Lahore, who belonged to all of India and none of it, thought: here is a way onto the road. He followed. Thus begins the Great Game, and the long walk, and the story inside the story.

"The Road and the Wheel are one. All things move. All things return. What you follow is what you become."
— The Lama, Kim, Rudyard Kipling
