Territory II

The Western Shores

From the Records of Robert Louis Stevenson

Squire Trelawney, Dr. Livesey, and the rest of these gentlemen having asked me to write down the whole particulars about Treasure Island, from the beginning to the end, keeping nothing back but the bearings of the island, and that only because there is still treasure not yet lifted, I take up my pen in the year of grace 17— and go back to the time when my father kept the Admiral Benbow inn and the brown old seaman with the sabre cut first took up his lodging under our roof.

I remember him as if it were yesterday, as he came plodding to the inn door, his sea-chest following behind him in a hand-barrow — a tall, strong, heavy, nut-brown man, his tarry pigtail falling over the shoulder of his soiled blue coat, his hands ragged and scarred, with black, broken nails, and the sabre cut across one cheek, a dirty, livid white. I remember him looking round the cover and whistling to himself as he did so, and then breaking out in that old sea-song that he sang so often afterwards:

“Fifteen men on the dead man’s chest —
Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!
Drink and the devil had done for the rest —
Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!”

In his broken, hoarse voice he sang those words — seemingly to himself, having looked about the inn from under his old tarred hat and liking, I suppose, the look of the place and the company. Then he rapped on the door with a bit of stick like a handspike that he carried, and when my father appeared, called roughly for a glass of rum. This, when it was brought to him, he drank slowly, like a connoisseur, lingering on the taste and still looking about him at the cliffs and up at our signboard.

“This is a handy cove,” he said at last; “and a pleasant sittyated grog-shop. Much company, mate?”

My father told him no, very little company, the more was the pity.

“Well, then,” said he, “this is the berth for me. Here you, matey,” he cried to the man who trundled the barrow; “bring up alongside and help up my chest. I’ll stay here a bit,” he continued. “I’m a plain man; rum and bacon and eggs is what I want, and that head up there for to watch ships off. What you mought call me? You mought call me captain.”

Territory III

The Dark Interior

The map is found — all changes

Not long after this, the captain came up behind me and laid his hand on my shoulder — he was often quiet after his first glass — and I could see from his eye that something had troubled him.

“Tell me, boy,” he said in a low tone, “does a seafaring man ever come in here to enquire after me? A seafaring man, mind ye. Something like me, but leaner and younger, maybe — like a lad at sea?”

I told him no; and he breathed again through his nose, and looked frightened. I said nothing, but he continued to ask me from time to time, and whenever a stranger came to the Admiral Benbow, he would look through the curtained door to see if it was such a person, but mostly when a stranger did appear it was not the person he sought, and he would relax, though still watchful.

The map that lay within the oilskin packet — the thing that would cost blood and sail and years of ocean — was no more at first than a crumpled paper, stained with sea-salt and something darker. But the latitude and longitude in faded red ink were precise as murder.

One January morning, very early — it was a bitter, foggy, frosty morning — the captain rose earlier than usual and set out down the beach, his cutlass swinging under the broad skirts of the old blue coat, his brass telescope under his arm, his hat tilted back upon his head. I remember his breath hanging like smoke in his wake as he strode off.

Mother was upstairs with father; and I was laying the breakfast table against the captain's return when the parlour door opened and a man stepped in on whom I had never set my eyes before. He was a pale, tallowy creature, wanting two fingers of the left hand, and though he wore a cutlass, he did not look much like a fighter. I had always my eye open for seafaring men, with one reason or another, and I remember this one puzzled me. He was not sailorly, and yet he had a smack of the sea about him too.

Territory IV

Here Be Monsters

The Cartographer's Warning

In the old cartographic tradition, the edges of the known world were populated not with blankness, but with imagination. Hic svnt dracones. Here be dragons. The sea monster was the cartographer’s admission that beyond a certain longitude, the map became story.

Storyteller inhabits that same frontier. Every session begins at the edge of the known — the blank margin of your world — and populates it with creatures of narrative necessity. The Narrator does not know what you will find. Neither do you. That is the point.

The Play Engine generates worlds the way the ancients filled their terra incognita: purposefully imprecise, emotionally resonant, structurally sound beneath the ornamentation. The monster in the margin is not an error. It is a promise.

The Guide corrects without interrupting. The Narrator narrates without apology. Storytime is never broken to explain the machinery. The machine serves the story, not the other way around.

In the sixteen-hundreds, Dutch cartographers would fill the oceans of their maps with illustrations of actual known sea creatures — whales, walruses, narwhals — alongside pure invention. The accuracy was beside the point. The intention was to communicate something true: the sea is full of things you have not imagined yet.

Storyteller says the same. Your adventure is full of things you have not imagined yet. We know where they are. We have drawn the map.
